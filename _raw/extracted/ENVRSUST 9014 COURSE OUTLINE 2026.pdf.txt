Course description

In Environmental Data Analysis and Data Visualization, we will focus on the sourcing,
scrutiny, and critical evaluation of environmental data. A major emphasis will be placed
on developing transferable and technical skills in data management, visualization,
statistical analysis, and interpretation. By working with datasets connected to real-world
environmental issues, students will build the skills needed to identify patterns, evaluate
evidence, communicate findings, and develop a deeper understanding of critical
environmental challenges.

This fast-paced course is designed to challenge your problem-solving and analytical
skills through a mix of individual and group assessments. Before each class, you will
complete short technical exercises designed to build your confidence with
environmental datasets and analytical workflows. In class, we will focus on applying
these skills to real-world environmental problems, with an emphasis on hands-on
practice, data interpretation, and clear communication.

Creativity will be encouraged throughout the course, particularly through an
independent assignment that will challenge students to use their acquired technical
expertise to create a final StoryMap and presentation on a current environmental issue.
Students will be expected to source, analyze, visualize, and interpret data in a way that
is both technically sound and accessible to a broader audience.

Through our exploration of environmental data, we will cover a broad range of
environmental topics, including biodiversity loss, invasive species, air and water
pollution, land use change, climate change, and environmental monitoring. Across
these topics, the focus will remain on how data can be used to understand complex
environmental challenges, evaluate uncertainty, and communicate meaningful insights.

Learning outcomes
By the end of the course, students will be able to:

   •   Apply interdisciplinary data analysis approaches to investigate complex
       environmental problems by integrating field, statistical, geospatial, and open-
       source datasets to interpret patterns, evaluate evidence, and communicate
       findings.

   •   Develop problem-solving, decision-making, and critical analysis skills within the
       context of working with and presenting environmental data.

   •   Conduct environmental field data collection and integrate field observations with
       statistical, geospatial, and open-source datasets to support environmental
       assessment and decision-making.

Course Structure

This course is organized into two complementary units. In Unit 1, we focus on
developing a broad suite of data analysis skills across multiple software platforms. Skills
are introduced incrementally, emphasizing common analytical concepts and workflows
across tools (e.g., joins in Excel and R alongside spatial joins in QGIS). This approach
allows us to build technical proficiency efficiently while developing a versatile toolkit for
data analysis. Unit 1 concludes with a series of examinations at the end of October to
assess your core competencies and technical skills. In Unit 2, students apply their
acquired skills through two major projects:
   •   Independent Project StoryMap – students conduct an extensive exploratory
       data analysis by sourcing openly available datasets, organizing them, and
       analyzing them to generate evidence-based insights into their focal
       environmental area.
   •   National Pollutant Release Inventory (NPRI) Challenge – our class will work in
       partnership with the NPRI team at Environment and Climate Change Canada to
       answer pressing questions about releases across the country.

Pre-class work

Our class uses a partially flipped format. In a flipped classroom, you complete
preparatory work before arriving, which helps make class time more engaging and
effective. For each session, you will be expected to complete pre-class work before the
lesson begins. Once you arrive to class, you will answer a short quiz on the pre-class
work to assess your understanding and then we will use our in-person time to reinforce
key concepts and focus on hands-on, practical application.


Before each class, you will complete work under the following categories:


   •   Video tutorials (found under Course topics and training): In each class, we will
       develop a set of practical technical skills used in environmental data analysis.
       Before arriving to class, you will work through a group of topic videos that guide
       you step by step through the tools and techniques we will use that day. In class,
       you will apply these skills to new datasets, using them to explore practical
       questions and draw insights from environmental data.

   •   Readings (located in pre-class work under each class): In each class, we will be
       working through a real dataset. To help you understand the dataset we will be
       using, you may be assigned a short selection of readings on that topic. These will
       include scientific articles, government reports, and news stories, providing
       background and real-world context for our in-class discussions. The readings are
       designed to help you engage with key issues and provide context for our in-class
       data analysis.

Independent Project StoryMap (30%)

The major project for this course is an independent exploratory data analysis
assignment where you will study a topic of your choice. This project is intentionally
open-ended, offering you the flexibility to define a reasonable scope, source relevant
datasets, and conduct meaningful analyses. It challenges you to thoughtfully plan and
execute your data exploration from start to finish. A defining aspect of this project is the
sourcing of data from open-source databases and repositories. You'll take the lead in
combining and analyzing datasets to uncover meaningful relationships and answer the
key questions you have identified for your project. You will select a broad environmental
issue and evaluate it through the exploration of temporal, spatial, and other relevant
environmental data.

The final deliverable for your project is a comprehensive ArcGIS storymap that
highlights the results of your analysis and provides interpretation by effectively
integrating text, figures, maps, and tables into one flowing document. We have
scheduled an elective ArcGIS storymaps and dashboards workshop for you on
November 6th and unless you feel very confident using storymaps and dashboards
already, I would strongly recommend that you attend that workshop. Storymaps are a
fun and interactive way to share data visualizations (we’ll review examples throughout
the course) and this project will give you (1) practice on how to best use storymaps and
dashboards and (2) a final deliverable you can share with potential co-op employers.

For your storymap, you will be asked to include the following:

    •   Infographic - You will create a `social media ready` infographic that provides an
        overview and summary of your analysis for the public.
    •   Figures – You will need 10 figures created using R
    •   Maps - 10 maps created in QGIS (with at least two interactive maps)
    •   Dashboard - at least one dashboard
    •   Written summary - A written interpretation to accompany your data
        visualizations and analysis that summarizes your key findings. Make sure to cite
        all figures and maps that you include. When interpreting your results, incorporate
        references from academic literature to provide context; however, the emphasis
        should be primarily on your own analysis and interpretation of the open-source
        data you have gathered.
    •   A list of data sources - Provide a list of data sources you used for your project
        with weblinks to each of them.

        assignment. Use APA 7th edition for your reference format.

All figures, graphs, or maps should be created (by you) from data you have collected
from various open sources. Please note, you will also submit your R scripts as part of

Indepdent Project Presentation (5%)

On November 23rd or November 24th, you will present to the class covering your main
findings. You will have an opportunity to take on feedback from this session before
submitting your final StoryMap. Your presentation will be about 10 minutes, with time for
questions afterwards. You will use a slide deck to take us through your work rather than
presenting directly from your StoryMap, and you should ensure it includes the
following:

   •    Title slide – A title for your project and your name
   •    Introduction slides (one or two) – Provide some basic background information
        about the topic.
            o What was the issue you explored?
            o Why did you explore that issue?
            o What is currently understood about it?
            o Where did you find the data for your project?
   •    Content slides – A range of slides showing some of your most compelling figures
        and maps. You should explain each of the figures and the interpretation within
        the context of your overall project. Please note, you do not have to share all your
        figures or maps and should focus on the figures that you find most impactful.


National Pollutant Release Inventory (NPRI) Assignment (20%)

We are collaborating with Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) to answer
pressing research questions about Canada’s non-GHG emissions by using data from
the National Pollutant Release Inventory. You will work in pre-assigned groups of five to
six students to create a report for ECCC on various pre-defined topics.

You will submit a written report to your ECCC mentor for review that includes figures,
maps and tables and a write-up on your findings. You can find more detailed guidance
and past examples of reports on our NPRI Group Assignment page on OWL
Brightspace.

Note: You will receive feedback from your ECCC mentor and your team will be
responsible for responding to that feedback and submitting the final copy back to

ECCC. You will submit this finalized copy with track changes for a portion of your
assignment grade.

NPRI Presentation (5%)

You will also give a virtual presentation to the ECCC mentors at the beginning of class
on December 2nd. Each group will present detailed findings from their NPRI analysis,
focusing on their assigned topic area and the key patterns, trends, or concerns
identified through their data work. The presentation should translate your analysis into
clear, evidence-based insights for the ECCC team and should highlight the figures,
maps, tables, or other visualizations that best support your interpretation.

Where appropriate, groups should also identify potential recommendations that emerge
from their findings. These may include suggestions for improved monitoring (e.g.,
changes in monitoring thresholds), areas where further investigation may be warranted,
or priority areas that could help guide future analysis and decision-making. The goal is
to present your work in a professional format that is useful to an applied audience and
demonstrates how NPRI data can support environmental monitoring and policy-relevant
decision-making.

Class norms

Attendance at all sessions in this course is mandatory, and it is your responsibility to
advise the instructor and seek assistance if you are unable to contribute to class
discussions. Circumstances may arise that make it impossible for you to attend,
including health-related reasons. If you are unable to meet a course requirement due to
illness or other serious circumstances, please contact the instructor.

Under University regulations, I can determine at what point absenteeism has become
excessive and thus may prevent you from passing the course. Any student who has not
attended enough sessions to fully master the material may not be permitted to receive
credit for the course.

be provided for individual assessments.

Plagiarism

Students must write their essays and assignments in their own words. Whenever
students take an idea, or a passage from another author, they must acknowledge their
debt both by using quotation marks where appropriate and by proper referencing such
as footnotes or citations. Plagiarism is a major academic offence (see Scholastic
Discipline for Graduate Students in the Western Academic Calendar).

All required papers may be subject to submission for textual similarity review to the
commercial plagiarism detection software under license to the University for the
detection of plagiarism. All papers submitted will be included as source documents in
the reference database for the purpose of detecting plagiarism of papers subsequently
submitted to the system. Use of the service is subject to the licensing agreement,
currently between Western University and Turnitin.com (hyperlink www.turnitin.com).

Accessible Education (AE)



Western is committed to achieving barrier-free accessibility for all its members,
including graduate students. As part of this commitment, Western provides a variety of
services devoted to promoting, advocating, and accommodating persons with
disabilities in their respective graduate program. Graduate students with disabilities (for
example, chronic illnesses, mental health conditions, mobility impairments) are strongly
encouraged to register with Accessible Education (AE), a confidential service designed
to support graduate and undergraduate students through their academic program. With
the appropriate documentation, the student will work with both AE and their graduate
programs (normally their Graduate Chair and/or Course instructor) to ensure that
appropriate academic accommodations to program requirements are arranged. These
accommodations may include individual counselling, alternative formatted literature,
accessible campus transportation, learning strategy instruction, writing exams and
assistive technology instruction.


Accommodation Policies

provides recommendations for accommodation based on medical documentation or
psychological and cognitive testing. The policy on Academic Accommodation for
