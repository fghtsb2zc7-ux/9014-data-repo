Chapter 1 - overview of the NPRI program

•   The NPRI is Canada’s Pollutant Release
    and Transfer Register.

•   It falls under the Canadian
    Environmental Protection Act, 1999
    (CEPA), and is implemented by
    Environment and Climate Change
    Canada (ECCC).

•   The NPRI covers releases,
    disposals and transfers
    for recycling of over
    491 pollutants from over 7,000 facilities.

•   The NPRI provides tools and guidance to
    assist facilities with reporting.




3
    History of the NPRI
•    Reporting requirements were established in 1992 by a multi-
     stakeholder advisory committee, with representatives from federal,
     provincial and territorial governments, industry and environmental
     organization.

•    Original NPRI list of substances was derived through stakeholder
     consultation.
      •   A large measure of consensus was achieved, with several items to be
          discussed as the program develops.

•    In 1993, the first reporting year, 178 substances were listed.




4
Starting point: Toxics Release
Inventory substance list
•   The United States’ Toxics Release Inventory (TRI) substance list from 1990 was
    used as a starting point.

•   In 1990, the Toxics Release Inventory listed about 320 substances/groups of
    substances that cause:

     •   Cancer or other chronic human health effects
     •   Significant adverse acute human health effects
     •   Significant adverse environmental effects

•   Substances included persistent organic pollutants, metals, ozone-depleting
    substances, pesticides, etc.

•   A number of substances were removed from consideration – those that were
    not used in Canada, determined using Canada’s Domestic Substances List.

Which substances are reported?
    Part      Reporting criteria
    Part 1A   Core Substances that must be reported if they are manufacture, processed or
              otherwise used, referred to as MPO for short, in amounts exceeding the reporting
              thresholds ( ≥10 tonnes at 1% concentration).

    Part 1B   Substances with alternate reporting threshold that must be reported when they are
              MPO at lower reporting concentration or in smaller quantities.

    Part 1C   These substances are per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). PFAS are used in
              many commercial applications and industrial sectors and are found in a wide range
              of products.
    Part 2    Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) with a higher threshold for grouped PAHs
              and a smaller threshold for individual PAHs.


    Part 3    Dioxins, furans and hexachlorobenzene produced by specific activities, mainly as by-
              producsts of industrial and combustion processes.


    Part 4    Criteria Air Contaminants (CACs). These substances are produced primarily by
              processes such as combustion, incineration and other industrial processes and
              contribute to air pollution issues such as acid rains and smog.


    Part 5    These are individual or speciated volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that must be
8             reported when releases to the air in a quantity ≥ 1 tonne.
    Who needs to report to the NPRI?
Reporting to the NPRI is mandatory under the Canadian Environmental
Protection Act, 1999 (CEPA). Reports are due as indicated in the
Canada Gazette. Owners and operators of facilities that meet the NPRI
reporting requirements published in the Canada Gazette, Part I, must
report to the NPRI.
In general, facilities are required to report to the NPRI if:

•       the total number of hours worked at the facility exceeded the 20,000-hour
        threshold (approximately 10 full-time employees)

•       they are engaged in certain activities (for example: wood preservation,
        incineration or burning fuels in stationary combustion equipment)

•       they manufacture, process, use or release NPRI substances in quantities
        above the reporting thresholds

What information is not reported to the NPRI?
The NPRI does not provide a comprehensive picture of all sources of pollution in
Canada. However, it does paint a general portrait of pollutant releases over time.

• The NPRI does not collect data on nonpoint source emissions. The Air Pollutant
  Emissions Inventory (APEI) produces reports that include additional sources
  such as transportation and agriculture.

• Not all Canadian facilities are required to report to the NPRI (e.g., they don't
  meet the thresholds).

• Not all substances are listed on the NPRI. Some substances not found on the
  NPRI may be regulated by other ECCC programs, including the:

       • Greenhouse Gas Reporting Program (GHGRP);
       • Air Pollutant Emissions Inventory (APEI);
       • Black Carbon Emissions Inventory.

  Making changes to NPRI reporting
  requirements
The NPRI substance list, reporting requirements, and supplementary
information are reviewed regularly. Anyone can submit a request to review
NPRI reporting requirements.

1. For a substance:
     • Is it manufactured, processed or otherwise used in Canada?
     • Is it of health and/or environmental concern?
     • Do facilities contribute significant releases of the substance?
     • Does its inclusion support one or more of the objectives of the NPRI?

2. For modification of reporting thresholds:
     • Science-based justification;
     • Capability of facilities to provide the required information;
     • Evidence of the significance of releases.

  Data collection: how to report to the NPRI?
Facilities that meet the NPRI’s reporting requirements must complete a
comprehensive report on their pollutant releases, disposals and transfers
using the online reporting system which is part of Environment and Climate
Change Canada's Single Window (SW).
• National Pollutant Release Inventory
• Greenhouse Gas Reporting
• Mine Effluent Reporting System
• Effluent Regulatory Reporting Information System
• Canadian Notification and Movement Tracking System
• Pollution Prevention Planning Notices
• Environmental Emergency Regulations Reporting System

 Quality control of data
• Data assurance and quality control are performed by:

      • NPRI employees;
      • Environnement and Climate Change Canada sector experts;
      • Industry associations

• Facilities are regularly encouraged to verify their own data.

• Examples of common reporting errors observed during quality control
  activities:

      •   Using inappropriate reporting units and decimal errors:
      •   Omitting a substance or the source of a release;
      •   Using incorrect reference data or assumptions for calculations;
      •   Forgetting the (-) sign in the facility’s geographical coordinates.

    Uses of NPRI data
•    To identify pollution prevention priorities;
•    To support the assessment and management of risks from chemicals,
     e.g. under the Chemicals Management Plan (CMP), and air quality
     modelling;
•    To complement monitoring data from other programs, e.g. water and
     air quality monitoring.
•    To encourage actions to reduce releases of pollutants into the
     environment;
•    To improve public understanding and support research on pollutants;
•    To support annual international reporting.

Public “right-to-know” is a fundamental underlying principle of the NPRI.
